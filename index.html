Scaling convention: By default, K scales as an illustrative active fraction of 2% of N (minimum 1). This is a demonstration rule to keep the slider meaningful across scales, not a claim about biological scaling.

What the number C(N, K) means—and what it doesn't

A neuron integrates inputs arriving through many synapses. Different input ensembles may contribute to the generation of the same outgoing spike.

The mathematical number C(N, K) counts distinct sets of K active synapses chosen from N available synapses.

It does not mean all these combinations naturally occur, are equally probable, or are sufficient to trigger an action potential. The real neuron is sensitive to synaptic weights, dendritic locations, inhibition, timing, membrane state, and other physiological conditions.

Why brute-force enumeration is a DEAD END

DEAD END refers to attempting to explicitly construct and store every theoretical combination using brute-force enumeration. It is not a claim that biological neurons perform such enumeration.

Even if you slide N down from 10,000 to just 1,000 synapses with 20 active inputs, C(1,000, 20) still exceeds 3.39 × 1041 combinations. Exhaustive lookup tables are impossible in both tissue and silicon.

How does biological computation operate within this enormous potential input space without enumerating it?

Biological Evidence — Experimental Neuroscience Supporting the Demonstration (Click to expand)

Our starting point of 10,000 synapses and 200 simultaneous activations is a deliberately simplified illustration, not a universal biological constant. Below are key experimental and modeling studies on synaptic counts, coincidence detection, and nonlinear dendritic subunits. Note the distinction between model-derived predictions and direct experimental measurements.

Eyal et al. (2018) — Human Cortical Pyramidal Neurons: From Spines to Spikes via Models

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6034553/

Anatomically and physiologically constrained computational models of human cortical pyramidal neurons.

  • Anatomical estimate: 20,000–30,000 dendritic spines on studied human layer 2/3 pyramidal cells.
  • Model-derived prediction: Approximately 134 ± 28 simultaneous excitatory synaptic activations for a somatic action potential with 50% probability.
  • Model-derived prediction: Approximately 20 ± 10 appropriately clustered activations could trigger local dendritic NMDA spikes in the model.

Azouz & Gray (2000) — Dynamic spike threshold reveals a mechanism for synaptic coincidence detection in cortical neurons in vivo

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC16678/

Experimental measurement: Demonstrates that cortical spike threshold depends on the rate of preceding membrane depolarization, supporting sensitivity to the temporal coincidence of incoming signals.

Polsky, Mel & Schiller (2004) — Computational subunits in thin dendrites of pyramidal cells

https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1253

Experimental measurement: Shows that inputs clustered on the same thin dendritic branch can integrate nonlinearly, while spatially separated inputs behave differently.

Losonczy & Magee (2006) — Integrative Properties of Radial Oblique Dendrites in Hippocampal CA1 Pyramidal Neurons

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16630839/

Experimental measurement: Patterned synaptic stimulation showed that appropriately timed inputs can produce local dendritic spikes, illustrating the importance of coincidence and spatial organization.

Spruston (2008) — Pyramidal neurons: dendritic structure and synaptic integration

https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2286

Review: Explains dendritic organization, synaptic integration, coincidence detection, and the specialized computational functions of dendritic branches.
